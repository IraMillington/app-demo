Health · Expo / React Native · iPhone

Food, habits and training, with a camera that counts

Health is a food, habit, symptom and training tracker for two people in one household. It pulls steps, energy and sleep from Apple Health or Fitbit, and conditions picked at setup (lactose intolerance, IBS, high blood pressure and more) change what it tracks and which nutrients it weighs.

What makes it different

  • Up to four photos, one meal. A top-down shot and a side shot sharpen the portion estimate. The model is told twice that the photos are one meal, so two angles never count as two servings.
  • The app reshapes itself around conditions. Lactose intolerance adds lactose in grams per meal against 6 g and 12 g limits, with a lactase check-off. Gut conditions add a 1–5 “how did it sit” rating for each meal and a monthly trigger analysis. Sam has none; Settings shows what each one would change.
  • An empty meal can mean something. “Didn’t eat this meal” records a real skipped meal, so a gap in the diary isn’t mistaken for a day you barely ate and felt fine.
  • A workout program that runs itself. Rotating workouts, a built-in logger with a rest timer and last time's weights, phases that change the effort target, and a progress chart for every lift.
  • A household leaderboard for steps, sleep, getting up and public habits.

The AI inside, and why these models

  • Meal estimates: Gemini 3.8 Flash. Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite was chosen first, on quality per dollar, but it could not judge portions. Shown a photo of a leftover pizza quarter, it said a quarter, three quarters and seven eighths had been eaten on different runs (500 to 3,500 kcal). 3.8 Flash said three quarters every time, for about a cent more per estimate.
  • Fallback: Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite. Qwen was tried first. In a 20-plate Nutrition5k test it was rate-limited on 9 of 14 calls and took about 54 seconds on the rest; Lite answered every call in about 5 seconds with about the same calorie error. Claude and GPT models reject the strict answer format, so they cannot stand in.
  • Monthly trigger analysis: Claude Sonnet 5.5. It runs twelve times a year at 10 to 15 cents a run, and lands within a few points of Opus 5.5 on reasoning tests at half the price.
  • Models are a setting on the server, so they can be swapped without an app update. In this demo the AI answers are pre-written; everything the server does with them is real.

Claro · Expo / React Native · iPhone

Everyday Mexican Spanish for a returning beginner

Claro is a Spanish course built around real situations in Mexico: ordering at a fonda, calling the plumber, texting a friend. Units run from A1 to B2, with spaced reviews, graded readings, conversations and a hands-free “Just listen” mode for work. There is no account: progress lives on the phone.

What makes it different

  • FSRS review timing. The same scheduler serious flashcard apps use. After 100 delayed reviews Claro fits one personal multiplier to how you actually remember.
  • “Say it and check.” Speech is recognized as Spanish (Mexico) on the phone and compared word by word with the sentence. It measures whether you were understood, not your accent, and nothing is recorded.
  • A writing checker that knows beginner slips. Soy 26 años, yo gusto, gracias para, a missing ¿ or accent. Slip categories feed a “What to work on” card, never your own text.
  • Practice anywhere. The 🎧🎤 pill says whether you can listen or speak right now. Turn speaking off and speaking prompts become writing prompts.
  • Mexican usage, explained. What ahorita really means, tú versus usted at work, and why ¿me pasas la sal? sounds natural.

The AI inside, and why these models

  • Answer translator: Claude Haiku 5.5. The word-by-word check shows where a typed answer differs from the model sentence, but only a language model can say what the learner's own Spanish actually means. Haiku read beginner mistakes best of ten models tried on Oct 28, at about $0.00025 an answer. Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite, at the same price, is the fallback.
  • It only ever shows a translation and up to three notes. It never changes a rating or a score, it is sent only the prompt, your answer and the model answers, and it goes quiet offline. It is off in this demo.
  • No AI where a rule does better. Review timing is the FSRS algorithm, the writing checker is hand-written rules for known slips, and “Say it and check” uses the phone's own Spanish (Mexico) speech recognizer.

  1. 1On Today, tap Start to recall the phrases due now, then rate how well you knew each one. The next review date comes from FSRS.
  2. 2Open What to work on: the slips Sam keeps making, such as soy 26 años, with a short practice.
  3. 3On Practice, try Verb forms or the Sentence lab, or type an answer and use Check my writing.
  4. 4Tap the 🎧🎤 pill and turn speaking off: speaking prompts become writing prompts.
  5. 5Search Phrases and star a few you want to keep.
  6. 6On Progress, see what is staying with Sam after a delay, and tap See my week.

What is real. Both phones run the apps' own source code, unmodified, exported for the web with Expo. Claro keeps its state on the device, so it runs here as on the phone (only the optional answer translator, which needs the home server, is off); its demo learner was built by Claro's own scheduling and course code.

What is stood in. Health talks to a server at home. For this page, that real server ran against a throwaway database of invented people while a script tapped through this same build, and the phone replays those recorded answers. Your taps move the replay along, and settings you change are kept on the page; a change the script never made shows the closest recorded result instead. The AI photo estimates are canned answers that the server then processed for real. The browser has no camera roll, notifications or speech recognition, so those use small stand-ins.

Left out on purpose: tools without a phone screen (the Gmail organizer, the Spotify playlist jobs) and apps built for work or shared with someone else.
