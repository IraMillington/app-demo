iPhone apps built with AI, running live

  • Health, Recipes, Claro: personal apps for a household and friends, installed straight onto their iPhones, not sold in the App Store. Health is used every day by three people.
  • PickleCourts: a public court-booking app, co-developed, with 200+ App Store downloads, plus MCP tools that let AI assistants search and book courts.

  • Real code, invented data: each phone runs the same source that ships to the iPhone, built for the browser. The first three talk to each other the way the real apps do.

Everything here is invented: the people, meals, recipes, photos, workouts and scores. Nothing you do on this page is sent anywhere. The demo is set on Friday, November 6, the day its data was recorded.

How the three apps connect

Health is the hub. Recipes signs in with your Health account and sends meals to your Health diary; Claro sends the minutes you practice Spanish, which tick off a Health habit. Each app keeps its own data: Recipes has its own server and database, Claro keeps everything on the phone, and only these narrow links cross between them.

Recipes → Health

One login: a Health account signs straight into Recipes, and the diary is linked in the same step. Ingredients are matched to Health's food database, so recipes show nutrition per serving, and Save as a meal puts a serving one tap from the diary. Recipes never stores nutrition data.

Claro → Health

Claro sends the minutes you actually practiced, with a link code made from a Health habit; reaching the goal ticks it. The code can do only that one thing, and Claro never holds Health's API key. Its AI translator also runs on Health's server.

How AI is used, in building and in the apps

Built with coding agents

The apps, their servers, the home server and this page are written with Claude Code and Codex. Each repository has an AGENTS.md runbook the agents work from (how to test, deploy and roll back, and what never to touch), and tests decide whether a change ships: about 940 server tests for Health, plus tests for Recipes and Claro. This page rebuilds itself when an app changes, and refuses to publish if a scripted step fails, a server errors or a real name turns up in the recording.

Models picked by testing them

Health's photo model was chosen by scoring candidates on 20 Nutrition5k plates with known calories, and by rerunning one photo to check the answer holds still. Claro's translator was chosen from ten models given the same beginner answers. The details are under each app. Models are server settings, so they change without an app update.

Guardrails around every call

Replies must match a JSON schema, and requests only go to providers that enforce it. A failure falls back to a second model, then reports an error instead of guessing. Photos are never stored, and the translator can't change a score. Where a rule does better than a model (review timing, writing slips, unit conversion), there is no model.

Tools for AI agents: PickleCourts

On PickleCourts (co-developed): MCP booking tools, account linking and a plugin with a booking skill, so assistants can find and book courts safely. The PickleCourts tab shows the tools and their real answers on demo data.

Not built yet: a dedicated evaluation suite for these AI features. The model choices rest on the one-off tests above, and the plugin's reviewer test prompts (five that should trigger tools, three that shouldn't) are the start of one for booking.

Health · personal app · Expo / React Native · iPhone

Food, habits and training, with a camera that counts

Health is a food, habit, symptom and training tracker, used every day by three people. It pulls steps, energy and sleep from Apple Health or Fitbit, and conditions picked at setup (lactose intolerance, IBS, high blood pressure and more) change what it tracks and which nutrients it weighs.

What makes it different

  • Up to four photos, one meal. A close-up and a wider shot sharpen the portion estimate. The model is told twice that the photos are one meal, so two angles never count as two servings.
  • The app reshapes itself around conditions. Lactose intolerance adds lactose in grams per meal against 6 g and 12 g limits, with a lactase check-off. Gut conditions add a 1–5 “how did it sit” rating for each meal and a monthly trigger analysis. Sam has none; Settings shows what each one would change.
  • An empty meal can mean something. “Didn’t eat this meal” records a real skipped meal, so a gap in the diary isn’t mistaken for a day you barely ate and felt fine.
  • A workout program that runs itself. Rotating workouts, a built-in logger with a rest timer and last time's weights, phases that change the effort target, and a progress chart for every lift.
  • A household leaderboard for steps, sleep, getting up and public habits.

The AI inside

  • Meal photos: Gemini 3.8 Flash, with Gemini 3.5 Flash Lite as fallback. Picked Oct 20 by testing, not by reputation (details below). Replies must match a JSON schema; a failure tries the fallback, then shows an error rather than a guess.
  • Monthly trigger analysis: Claude Sonnet 5.5. Explained in full below.
  • Models are a server setting, swapped without an app update. In this demo the AI answers are pre-written; everything the server does with them is real.
How the photo models were tested
  • Accuracy. Each candidate estimated 20 plates from Nutrition5k, a research set of real cafeteria meals with known calories, scored on calorie error, speed and failed calls.
  • Consistency. The same leftover-pizza photo was run repeatedly. The cheaper model in use until then said a quarter, three quarters and seven eighths had been eaten on different runs (500 to 3,500 kcal). 3.8 Flash said three quarters every time, for about a cent more per estimate.
  • Fallback. The previous fallback, Qwen3.8 Flash, was rate-limited on 9 of 14 calls and took about 54 seconds on the rest. Flash Lite answered every call in about 5 seconds with about the same calorie error.

The monthly trigger analysis

For someone with a gut condition, the hard question is what keeps setting it off. Once a month Health hands a model the whole finished month as a dated timeline and asks for suspected triggers, each with its evidence, a confidence and its reasoning.

  1. What goes in. Every food with its time, lactose in grams, whether a lactase pill was taken, and inflammatory flags; each after-meal rating (“felt 2/5 forty-five minutes after dinner: bloating”); symptoms and severity; water against target; sleep; daily stress and mood notes; and menstrual cycle days.
  2. What it is told. Compare foods against the symptoms that follow them, but treat cycle phase as its own variable, so a flare around a period is not blamed on that day's lunch. A food that repeats as a trigger across different cycle phases is the strongest evidence. Only call something a trigger when the data supports it, and never frame it as a diagnosis.
  3. What comes back. A list of triggers, each with evidence, a low, medium or high confidence, a recommendation and the reasoning, forced into that shape by a strict JSON schema. It is saved and shown in the monthly report.
  4. What is left out. Days marked as not logged are dropped entirely, so a half-logged day can't look like a symptom-free one. A month still in progress is refused. It runs twelve times a year, at 10 to 15 cents a run.

Sonnet 5.5 was chosen Sept 28 from published reasoning scores rather than a test of our own: within a few points of Opus 5.5 at half the price. Sam has no gut conditions, so the analysis doesn't appear in this demo; Settings shows which conditions turn it on.

Recipes · personal app · Expo / React Native · iPhone

A cookbook shared with friends, linked to the food diary

Recipes is a social cookbook: everyone has a profile, adds friends and shares recipes with them, then saves or copies what friends share. It has its own FastAPI server and Postgres database beside Health's on the home server, with the same build, update and deploy setup.

What makes it different

  • Scale by what you have. “How much chicken do you have?” Type 1 lb instead of 1.5 and every amount follows, including the ones written inside the steps. US and metric measures sit side by side.
  • One visibility rule, checked in one place. A recipe is Only me, Friends or Everyone, and every read on the server goes through the same rule, so the feed, profiles and saves can't disagree about who sees what.
  • Save versus copy. Saving bookmarks the original and follows the owner's edits; copying gives you your own private version that remembers where it came from.
  • Nutrition from Health, not a second database. Ingredients are matched to Health's foods (its search lists foods you've logged before first), and a match is stored with the recipe, so a friend who copies it can log it too.

Claro · personal app · Expo / React Native · iPhone

Everyday Mexican Spanish for a returning beginner

Claro is a Spanish course built around real situations in Mexico: ordering at a fonda, calling the plumber, texting a friend. Units run from A1 to B2, with spaced reviews, graded readings, conversations and a hands-free “Just listen” mode for work. There is no account: progress lives on the phone. The one thing it sends is practice minutes, to a Health habit you link it to.

Why build it instead of using an existing app

  • Mexican Spanish, for real situations. General courses teach a neutral Spanish through set lessons. Claro teaches what is said in Mexico, in the places it is actually needed: the fonda, the plumber, a coworker, a text to a friend.
  • Built for a returning beginner. A placement check skips what is already known, and the units are about being understood, not about streaks, hearts or a daily quota.
  • It fits a workday. A hands-free listening mode for when speaking isn't possible, and a switch that turns every speaking prompt into writing.
  • It connects to the rest. Practice minutes tick off a habit in Health, and nothing is sent anywhere else: no account, no ads.

What makes it different

  • FSRS review timing. The same scheduler serious flashcard apps use. After 100 delayed reviews Claro fits one personal multiplier to how you actually remember.
  • “Say it and check.” Speech is recognized as Spanish (Mexico) on the phone and compared word by word with the sentence. It measures whether you were understood, not your accent, and nothing is recorded.
  • A writing checker that knows beginner slips. Soy 26 años, yo gusto, gracias para, a missing ¿ or accent. Slip categories feed a “What to work on” card, never your own text.
  • Practice anywhere. The 🎧🎤 pill says whether you can listen or speak right now. Turn speaking off and speaking prompts become writing prompts.
  • Mexican usage, explained. What ahorita really means, tú versus usted at work, and why ¿me pasas la sal? sounds natural.

The AI inside

  • Answer translator: Claude Haiku 5.5, picked Oct 28 from ten models. A word-by-word check shows where a typed answer differs; only a language model can say what the learner's own Spanish means. Each model read the same beginner answers, mistakes included. Haiku read them best, at about $0.00025 an answer.
  • It shows a translation and up to three notes, never changes a score, and runs on the Health server so no key ships in the app. It is off in this demo.
  • No AI where a rule does better. Review timing is the FSRS algorithm, the writing checker is hand-written rules for known slips, and “Say it and check” uses the phone's own Spanish (Mexico) speech recognizer.

PickleCourts · public, co-developed · Expo / React Native · iPhone and web

Book a pickleball court, from the app or from an AI assistant

PickleCourts is a public, co-developed court-booking marketplace with more than 200 downloads in the App Store. It shows open times across clubs that run different booking systems and books them in one checkout. This phone runs its consumer app against its real Node server and Postgres on a throwaway database. The clubs, prices and booking below are invented.

Tools for AI agents

  • An MCP server assistants can call. Venue search, account linking (OAuth with PKCE, rotating refresh tokens) and booking tools, so Claude, ChatGPT or any MCP client can find a court and book it.
  • Booking that can't go wrong twice. An assistant first gets a priced quote, and booking needs that quote's single-use confirmation token. It can't book twice, or book something you didn't confirm, and the tokens are revoked when you reset your password.
  • A plugin with a booking skill. A packaged plugin for ChatGPT and Claude carries a short skill that tells the assistant the steps: pin down place and date, search, offer two or three concrete times with prices, and never say a court is booked until checkout is finished.
  • Where it is. Search has been listed in Claude's connector directory since Oct 12, at https://api.picklecourts.rent/mcp. The ChatGPT plugin with booking is in OpenAI's review. Booking stays off in the Claude directory until payments are approved there.

What is real. All four phones run the apps' own source code, unmodified, exported for the web with Expo. Claro keeps its state on the device, so it runs here as on the phone (the optional answer translator, which needs the home server, is off); its demo learner was built by Claro's own scheduling and course code.

What is stood in. Health and Recipes talk to servers at home. For this page, those real servers ran against throwaway databases of invented people while a script tapped through these same builds, and the phones replay the recorded answers. The three phones share one recording, so a change in one shows up in the others. Your taps move the replay along, and settings you change are kept on the page; a change the script never made shows the closest recorded result instead. Reloading the page starts everything over. PickleCourts was recorded the same way against its own server, with payment in the server's mock mode, and keeps a separate replay; its other venues in the development data are real clubs, so they were hidden and three invented clubs added. The AI photo estimates are canned answers that the server then processed for real. The browser has no camera roll, notifications or speech recognition, so those use small stand-ins.
